\section{Application of Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) in
Stability Analysis and Genotype × Environment Interaction
Studies}

\textbf{Naser Sabaghnia}\footnote{\begin{quote}
  \textbf{Corresponding Author:} sabaghnia@maragheh.ac.ir
  \end{quote}}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{University of Maragheh, Iran}
\end{quote}

\textbf{Abstract:}

Genotype × environment interaction (GEI) is a major challenge in plant
breeding, as it complicates the identification of genotypes that are
both high-performing and stable across diverse environments.
Multi-environment trials (METs) generate complex datasets, often
unbalanced and incomplete, where traditional ANOVA or regression-based
stability metrics are limited. Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP),
within a mixed model framework, provides a robust approach for
estimating genotypic performance while accounting for random effects,
environmental variation, and heterogeneous residuals. BLUP enables
shrinkage-based predictions, improving accuracy over raw means or BLUE,
particularly in unbalanced datasets. Furthermore, incorporation of
pedigree or marker-derived relationship matrices allows exploitation of
genetic correlations, enhancing prediction of untested genotypes and
facilitating genomic selection. BLUP-based methods can also quantify
stability through variance of predicted genotypic values across
environments, factor-analytic decomposition of G×E, and
reliability-adjusted indices, providing actionable insights for
selection. This manuscript reviews theoretical foundations, practical
implementation, and computational approaches for BLUP in METs, including
hierarchical and factorial designs. It also discusses integration with
factor-analytic models for complex GEI, the use of combined pedigree and
marker information, and strategies for handling missing data. BLUP-based
stability analysis represents a flexible and powerful tool for modern
plant breeding, enabling selection of widely adapted and specifically
adapted genotypes while maximizing genetic gain under diverse
environmental conditions.

trials

\section{Introduction}

Plant breeders aim to select genotypes that combine high yield potential
with stability across diverse environments. Phenotypic performance of
genotypes is determined by their genetic makeup (G), environmental
conditions (E), and the interaction between genotype and environment
(G×E). While classical analysis of variance (ANOVA) partitions total
phenotypic variation into G, E, and GEI components, it does not provide
precise predictions of genotypic performance, especially in unbalanced
trials or when data from multiple environments and years are available
(Smith et al., 2001). Stability analysis is often employed to quantify
the consistency of genotype performance across environments. Classical
methods include the regression-based Finlay-Wilkinson approach, AMMI
(Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction) models, and
Eberhart-Russell parameters (Lynch \& Walsh, 1998). However, these
approaches have limitations in handling unbalanced data, missing
observations, and complex pedigree structures.

Mixed models and Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) have emerged as
powerful tools for addressing these limitations (Piepho \& Möhring,
2006). BLUP provides estimates of genotypic effects by explicitly
modeling random effects and variance-covariance structures,
accommodating unbalanced datasets, repeated measurements, and
heterogeneous variances. Furthermore, BLUP can incorporate pedigree or
marker-based genetic relationships, improving prediction accuracy for
selection in breeding programs. This manuscript reviews the theoretical
foundations of BLUP in the context of GEI and stability analysis,
discusses model formulation for hierarchical and factorial experimental
designs, and presents practical applications and considerations for
plant breeding programs.

\section{Stability Experiments}

Multi-environment trials are used to evaluate the performance of
genotypes across diverse environmental conditions. Typical designs
include randomized complete block designs (RCBD), incomplete block
designs, lattice designs, and factorial experiments. Trials are often
conducted over multiple years and locations to capture environmental
variability. Phenotypic data collected from METs are subject to both
spatial and temporal variation (Fig. 1). The statistical model for
phenotypic data is generally expressed as:

\[y_{ijk} = \mu + g_{i} + e_{j} + (ge)_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}
\]

BLUP is derived from the general linear mixed model (LMM):

\[\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta} + \mathbf{Zu} + \mathbf{\varepsilon}\mathbf{
}\]

\[\mathbf{u} \sim N(0,\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{\varepsilon} \sim N(0,\mathbf{R})\]

where \(\mathbf{G}\) represents the variance--covariance structure among
random effects, which can be simple (homogeneous) or complex
(heterogeneous or structured by pedigree or markers). \(\mathbf{R}\)
represents residual covariance, which may also accommodate spatial or
temporal correlation (Piepho \& Möhring, 2006).

The BLUP of \(\mathbf{u}\) is obtained by solving the mixed model
equations:

\[\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{X}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{X}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{Z} \\
\mathbf{Z}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{Z}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{G}^{- 1} \\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{\mathbf{\beta}} \\
\widehat{\mathbf{u}} \\
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{X}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{y} \\
\mathbf{Z}^{'}\mathbf{R}^{- 1}\mathbf{y} \\
\end{bmatrix}
\]

Genetic relationships among genotypes can be included via the numerator
relationship matrix (\(\mathbf{A}\)) or marker-based similarity matrices
(\(\mathbf{S}\)). The variance-covariance structure of genotypic effects
can be expressed as:

\[\mathbf{G} = \sigma_{g}^{2}\mathbf{A}\text{or}\mathbf{G} = \sigma_{g}^{2}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{
}\]

\[\mathbf{G} = r_{A}^{2}\mathbf{A} + r_{S}^{2}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{
}\]

BLUP can be applied to hierarchical (nested) and factorial designs:
Hierarchical (nested), so genotypes nested within families. Model allows
estimation of within- and between-family effects. Factorial (crossed),
like diallel crosses or hybrids from two heterotic pools. Model can
include general combining ability (GCA), specific combining ability
(SCA), and test-cross effects.

For example, a diallel model with GCA and SCA effects is expressed as:

\[y_{ijk} = \mu + g_{i} + g_{j} + s_{ij} + e_{k} + \varepsilon_{ijk}
\]

Stability is assessed by decomposing GEI effects or predicting genotype
performance across environments with low fluctuations (Fig. 2).
BLUP-based approaches included (i) BLUP of genotype-by-environment
effects which predicts genotypic performance in specific environments
(Schabenberger \& Gotway, 2005). (ii) Reliability-based stability
measures so every stability variance can be estimated using predicted
genotypic values. (iii) Factor-analytic models, which approximate G×E
covariance structure using latent factors; BLUPs of factor scores
provide stability estimates.

\section{SAS codes}

Regarding accept the assumptions of Model 1, the effect of the general
adaptability ability (GAA) of genotype i is equal to u+v\emph{i}. The
deviation from the sum u+v\emph{i} represents the specific adaptability
ability effect (SAA\emph{\textsubscript{ik}}) of genotype i in
environment k. This effect consists of a linear component (the effect of
environment dk) and a nonlinear component (the interaction effect
(vd)\textsubscript{ik}). To determine the effects of
GAA\textsubscript{i} and SAA\textsubscript{ik}, the mean phenotypic
values across replications are calculated. In this way, the results of
testing n genotypes across m environments can be arranged in an n×m
table, where: x\textsubscript{i} is the sum of all phenotypic values for
genotype i, x\textsubscript{k} is the total phenotypic sum in
environment k, X.. is the grand total of all phenotypic values,
x\textsubscript{ik} is the phenotypic value of genotype i in environment
k. The GAA and SAA effects are calculated using the following formulas
(Fig. 1):

\[x_{i.} = \sum_{k}^{}x_{ik} = x_{i1} + x_{i2} + \ldots + x_{im}\]

\[x_{nk} = \sum_{k}^{}x_{ik} = x_{1k} + x_{2k} + \ldots + x_{nk}\]

\[x_{ik} = u + v_{i} + d_{k} + {(vd)}_{ik}\]

\[x_{ik} = u + {GAA}_{i} + {SAA}_{ik}\]

The variance of random deviations for the mean phenotypic value in a
specific environment x\textsubscript{ik} is computed as:

\[{var}_{xik} = \frac{\sigma_{e}^{2}}{c} = \sigma^{2}\]

The variance of the difference between the means of two genotypes is
determined as:

\[{var}_{(x_{ik}/x_{nm})} = 2\sigma^{2}\]

To obtain the standard error of the differences between effects, the
square root of the corresponding variance must be taken. The least
significant difference (LSD) is then obtained by multiplying the
standard error by a constant magnitude. Comparison of genotypes in terms
of general adaptability can be performed by comparing
GAA\textsubscript{i} values. To estimate the interaction strength of
genotype i with environments, the genotype × environment interaction
variance can be used. To measure the stability of genotype i, it is
recommended to use the variance of SAA\textsubscript{i}. Therefore,
stability can be defined as the capacity of a genotype to maintain its
phenotypic expression under various environmental conditions through
organized mechanisms.

\section{Stability analysis}

Practical code examples for fitting BLUP in multi-environment trials
(METs), including stability analysis using factor-analytic models are:

proc mixed data=MET\_data;

class Genotype Environment Replicate;

model Yield = Environment / solution;

random Genotype Genotype*Environment;

/* Genotype and GxE effects as random */

lsmeans Genotype / pdiff cl;

run;

Genotype and Genotype*Environment are treated as random effects to
obtain BLUPs.

Environment is fixed to capture environmental means.

lsmeans Genotype provides predicted genotypic values across
environments.

Practical code examples for fitting BLUP in multi-environment trials
(METs), including

Factor-analytic model for G×E are:

proc mixed data=MET\_data method=REML;

class Genotype Environment;

model Yield = Environment;

random Genotype / type=fa(1) g;

repeated Environment / subject=Genotype type=ar(1);

ods output SolutionR=BLUPs;

run;

type=fa(1) specifies a factor-analytic covariance structure for G×E.

repeated allows modeling residual correlation across environments.

BLUPs output contains predicted genotypic values adjusted for G×E.

\section{Breeding for GAA while considering
stability}

The BLUP accounts for missing data, unbalanced replication, and
heterogeneous variance, yielding more accurate estimates of genotypic
performance across environments compared to classical ANOVA or
regression-based stability analysis. In factorial and diallel crosses,
BLUP allows estimation of GCA and SCA effects even for untested hybrids
(Bernardo, 1994). BLUP predictions enable the derivation of stability
metrics across environments, so variance of predicted genotype
performance across environments reflects stability. Also,
factor-analytic models separate main effects from interaction patterns,
highlighting genotypes with stable performance (low sensitivity to
environmental variation) or specific adaptation (high performance in
certain environments).

BLUP can model complex covariance structures among environments, such as
autoregressive or spatially correlated residuals (Edwards \& Jannink,
2006). Genotypic effects can be predicted for untested environments,
enabling selection for wide or targeted adaptation. Marker information
can enhance BLUP predictions by improving estimation of genetic
relationships, especially for genotypes with incomplete pedigree data.
Combined models of A and S matrices allow optimal utilization of both
pedigree and marker data.

BLUP provides a flexible, robust framework for analyzing MET data and
assessing GEI. Compared with classical stability metrics, BLUP improves
prediction of genotypic performance under unbalanced and incomplete data
(Fig. 3). Also, it accounts for complex variance-covariance structures,
including spatial, temporal, and pedigree effects. BLUP Supports
hierarchical and factorial designs, enabling dissection of GCA, SCA, and
within-family variation. Finally, it Facilitates integration of pedigree
and marker-based genetic relationships for genomic prediction.

The main assumptions of BLUP are normally distributed random effects and
correctly specified variance-covariance structures. In practice, REML
estimation is robust to moderate deviations from normality, although
outliers may affect inference (Piepho \& Möhring, 2006). Accurate
variance component estimation is crucial, particularly in plant
breeding, where the number of genotypes is often limited. Bayesian or
shrinkage approaches can help address heterogeneity among crosses or
environments (Edwards \& Jannink, 2006). BLUP-based stability analysis
also allows flexible interpretation of GEI (Schabenberger \& Gotway,
2005). Factor-analytic models and prediction-based stability metrics
provide information on general adaptability and specific adaptation,
guiding breeders in selecting genotypes for wide or targeted
environmental adaptation.

\section{Conclusion}

BLUP is a powerful tool for stability analysis and GEI studies in plant
breeding. By incorporating random effects, variance--covariance
structures, and pedigree or marker information, BLUP enables precise
prediction of genotypic performance across environments. Its ability to
handle unbalanced and incomplete data, coupled with flexible model
specification, makes it superior to classical methods in
multi-environment trials. The integration of BLUP with genomic
information and hierarchical experimental designs further enhances
selection efficiency. Future research should focus on robust variance
estimation in small datasets, robustness to non-normality, and practical
applications in modern breeding programs.
