{\Large \bf Beyond 1D Heuristics: Temporal-Causal Calibration and
Multivariate Non-Parametric Hypothesis Testing for Uncertainty
Quantification in Retrieval-Augmented Language Models}\\
{\bf
Sepehr Nazeri$^{1}$,
Dr. Ferdos Gorji$^{1}$\footnote[2]{\textbf{Corresponding Author:}
\texttt{f.gorji@aut.ac.ir}}\\
$^{1}$Department of Mathematics and Computer Science,
Amirkabir University of Technology}
\end{center}

\rule{\textwidth}{0.2mm}\\

\noindent\textbf{Abstract:}\\
Most methods for measuring how confident a language model should be
rely on a single number, predictive entropy, which is too simple to
capture what really happens when a model's built-in knowledge conflicts
with facts from an external source. We propose the Temporal-Causal
Calibration (TCC) framework, which measures \textit{parametric
stubbornness} - how strongly a model clings to old knowledge when new
evidence disagrees with it, using tools from causal inference. We then
use statistical hypothesis tests to check whether the model's
uncertainty scores are trustworthy. Tested on two small, locally run
models, TCC-UQ achieves an AUROC of $0.4895$ and an Expected Calibration
Error (ECE) of $0.1337$, using only 2.1\,MB of extra memory.
Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests ($D = 0.713$, $p \approx 4.90\times10^{-37}$)
and paired $t$-tests ($t = -64.48$, $p \approx 9.66\times10^{-111}$)
confirm that TCC-UQ produces a statistically distinct confidence
distribution from the uncalibrated baseline. The results demonstrate that causal grounding of stubbornness measurement
yields interpretable, statistically verifiable uncertainty signals, while
also revealing important limitations that motivate future work.\\
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation; Uncertainty Quantification(UQ);
Calibration; Non-Parametric Testing; Causal Inference.\\

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) helps language models give more
accurate answers by looking up relevant documents at query time, which
reduces outdated or made-up responses~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}. However,
adding an external retrieval step does not make the model more certain
, it actually makes uncertainty harder to understand. When a retrieved
document says something different from what the model already ``knows,''
it is not clear whether the model will trust the new document or stick
with its old knowledge. Current uncertainty methods deal with this by computing a single score,
such as the entropy over predicted tokens. This one-number approach
mixes together two different kinds of uncertainty, \emph{aleatoric
uncertainty}, which comes from genuine noise or ambiguity in the
retrieved text, and \emph{epistemic uncertainty}, which reflects gaps
in the model's knowledge, and also misses a third kind: the model's
tendency to ignore new evidence when it contradicts what the model
already believes. This paper makes three contributions to address this gap.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We give a precise definition of \emph{parametric stubbornness}
    using causal inference and show how to measure it from just two
    carefully chosen queries.
  \item Instead of a single diagnostic number, we use a set of
    non-parametric statistical tests, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and paired
    $t$-tests, applied to the full distribution of uncertainty scores.
  \item On two small, locally run models, we evaluate the resulting
    TCC-UQ framework against competing methods on AUROC, ECE, and
    Prediction Rejection Ratio (PRR), and provide a transparent
    discussion of both its strengths and current limitations.
\end{enumerate}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Statistical Foundations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Calibration and Expected Calibration Error}

A model that outputs a confidence score $\hat{p}$ is called
\emph{perfectly calibrated} if, among all the times it says a prediction
has probability $c$, exactly a fraction $c$ of those predictions turn
out to be correct:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\!\bigl(Y=1 \mid \hat{p}(Y=1 \mid X)=c\bigr) = c
  \quad \forall\, c\in[0,1].
\label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
Because (\ref{eq:calibration}) cannot be checked directly, we estimate
how well-calibrated a model is using the \emph{Expected Calibration
Error}~\cite{naeini2015obtaining}. We divide the confidence range $[0,1]$
into $M$ equal bins, and for each bin $B_m$ we compare the average
predicted confidence to the actual fraction of correct answers:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} =
  \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}
  \bigl|\overline{\mathrm{conf}}(B_m) - \overline{\mathrm{acc}}(B_m)\bigr|,
  \label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the total number of test examples,
$\overline{\mathrm{conf}}(B_m)$ is the average predicted confidence in
bin $m$, and $\overline{\mathrm{acc}}(B_m)$ is the fraction of correct
predictions in that bin. A perfectly calibrated model gives
$\mathrm{ECE}=0$; larger values mean the confidence scores are
misleading.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and AUROC}

In safety-critical applications, a model should be allowed to say
``I am not sure'' and refuse to answer rather than guess. The Area Under
the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) measures how well
the model's uncertainty score separates correct answers from wrong ones:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}\bigl(\mathrm{FPR}^{-1}(t)\bigr)\,dt,
\end{equation}
where TPR is the true-positive rate and FPR is the false-positive rate,
both computed as the uncertainty threshold varies. An AUROC of $1$ means
the model perfectly knows when it is right or wrong; $0.5$ is no better
than a random guess.

\subsection{Temporal-Causal Calibration Uncertainty Quantification (TCC-UQ)}

Consider a query $\mathbf{Q}$ that can be answered using either a
current document $\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{new}}$ or an outdated one
$\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{old}}$. Following Pearl's causal framework, we
distinguish between simply observing a context and actively
\emph{forcing} the model to use it, written $do(\mathbf{R})$, because
a model may pay more attention to some contexts than others. We define \emph{parametric stubbornness} $\Psi$ as the share of the
model's confidence that does \emph{not} change when we switch from the
outdated to the current context:
\begin{equation}
  \Psi = \frac{\hat{p}\bigl(Y\mid do(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{old}})\bigr)}
       {\hat{p}\bigl(Y\mid do(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{new}})\bigr)}
  \label{eq:stubbornness}
\end{equation}
When $\Psi \approx 0$ the model fully updates its answer to match the
new document; when $\Psi \approx 1$ the model largely ignores it. We
then define a corrected confidence score that scales down the raw
confidence by the stubbornness factor:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{\mathrm{TCC}}
  = \hat{p}\bigl(Y\mid do(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{new}})\bigr)
    - \hat{p}\bigl(Y\mid do(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{old}})\bigr)
  \label{eq:safe_conf}
\end{equation}
In our experiment, the model gave confidence $0.9000$ both when shown
the current document and when shown the outdated one. This gives
$\Psi = 1.0000$ (maximal stubbornness) and a corrected confidence of
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{TCC}} = 0.0000$, reflecting that the model was not
influenced at all by which document it was given. This extreme result
indicates that \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} did not update its outputs in
response to temporal context changes, which directly contributes to
the AUROC and PRR observed for TCC-UQ in this experiment.

\section{Multivariate Non-Parametric Hypothesis Testing}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Testing Framework}

Each method we compare produces a vector of uncertainty scores
$\mathbf{u}^{(k)} = (u_1^{(k)},\ldots,u_d^{(k)}) \in [0,1]^d$ for
every test example. Standard statistical tests assume these vectors
follow a normal distribution,
$\mathbf{u}^{(k)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$,
but in practice, uncertainty estimation in free-form language generation must account for semantic equivalences and variable-length outputs~\cite{kuhn2023semantic}, meaning standard parametric assumptions often do not hold. We therefore use tests that make no distributional assumption:

\paragraph{Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test.}
Given the score distributions $F$ and $G$ of two methods, the KS
statistic measures the largest gap between their cumulative distribution
functions:
\begin{equation}
  D_{nm} = \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|F_n(x)-G_m(x)\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $F_n$ and $G_m$ are estimated directly from the data. A large
$D_{nm}$ with a small $p$-value means the two methods produce
genuinely different score distributions. For large sample sizes $n$ and
$m$, the rescaled statistic $\sqrt{nm/(n+m)}\,D_{nm}$ follows the
Kolmogorov distribution under the null hypothesis $H_0: F = G$.

\paragraph{Paired $t$-test on rank-ordered scores.}
We sort the scores of both methods on the same test examples and apply
a paired $t$-test to the resulting ranks. Using ranks instead of raw
scores makes the test valid even when the scores are not normally
distributed, as long as the distributions are continuous and the pairs
are exchangeable.

\paragraph{Prediction Rejection Ratio.}
The PRR measures how useful the uncertainty score is when the model is
allowed to abstain on uncertain cases:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PRR}(\tau) =
  \frac{\mathrm{Acc}(\tau) - \mathrm{Acc}(0)}
       {1 - \mathrm{Acc}(0)},
\end{equation}
where $\tau \in [0,1]$ is the uncertainty threshold for abstention, $\mathrm{Acc}(\tau)$ is the accuracy on the examples the model
answers (those with uncertainty below $\tau$), and $\mathrm{Acc}(0)$ is
the accuracy when the model answers everything. A PRR of $1$ means the
uncertainty score perfectly flags wrong answers.

\section{Experimental Results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Setup}

All experiments ran on a single local machine using \texttt{qwen2.5:3b}
as the main model and \texttt{tinyllama:latest} as a smaller helper
model. We built an evaluation set of 150 question-and-context pairs,
each with a known correct answer, and varied the context between current
and outdated versions to test how the models responded.

Table~\ref{tab:performance} compares TCC-UQ against four existing
methods. Among the baselines, Textual Bayes achieves the best overall
profile with the highest AUROC ($0.8924$) and the lowest ECE ($0.0542$).
TCC-UQ in the current experiment yields an AUROC of $0.4895$, an ECE of
$0.1337$, and a PRR of $0.000$, indicating that the model's confidence
outputs were not informative for selective prediction on this evaluation
set. This outcome is directly explained by the measured stubbornness
$\Psi = 1.0$: because \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} produced identical confidence
values regardless of whether the current or outdated context was
supplied, the TCC correction collapsed to a fixed output and lost
discriminative power. The reliability diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:ece} shows the calibration
behaviour of TCC-UQ visually. TCC-UQ confidence scores are consistently
above the perfect-calibration diagonal in the observed bins (roughly
$0.4$--$0.75$), meaning the model is systematically overconfident in
this range. The ECE of $0.1337$ quantifies this gap.
Figure~\ref{fig:auroc} shows the ROC curve for TCC-UQ. The curve tracks
close to the diagonal reference line, consistent with an AUROC of
$0.4895$, which is near the random-guess baseline of $0.5$. This
reflects the loss of discriminative signal caused by the model's maximal
stubbornness ($\Psi = 1.0$) during this experimental run.

\subsection{Non-Parametric Statistical Audit}

Figure~\ref{fig:stat_curves} plots the confidence score distributions
of TCC-UQ and the uncalibrated baseline side by side. TCC-UQ scores
are shifted toward lower values (concentrated in the $0.4$--$0.55$
range) compared to the baseline (shifted toward $0.7$--$0.9$),
reflecting the stubbornness-based downward correction applied by the
TCC pipeline.

  \begin{center}
    \vspace{-20pt} 
    \makebox[\textwidth][c]{
      \makebox[0.45\textwidth][c]{\large\textbf{(A)}} \hfill
      \makebox[0.45\textwidth][c]{\large\textbf{(B)}}

    \includegraphics[width=1.00\textwidth]{main//p.1239//fig_statistical_validation_curves.png}
    \caption{(A) Empirical CDFs for TCC-UQ and the uncalibrated

      (B) Score densities: TCC-UQ scores are shifted lower than the
      baseline, confirming the stubbornness correction is active.}
    \label{fig:stat_curves}
  \end{center}

The KS test gives $D = 0.713$ ($p \approx 4.90\times10^{-37}$), which
strongly rejects the hypothesis that both methods produce the same score
distribution. The paired $t$-test gives $t = -64.48$
($p \approx 9.66\times10^{-111}$), confirming that TCC-UQ confidence
scores are systematically lower than the baseline scores across all test
examples. Both tests reject the null hypothesis at every standard
significance level ($\alpha \in \{0.05, 0.01, 0.001\}$), even after
applying the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The
negative $t$-statistic indicates that the TCC correction consistently
reduces confidence relative to the uncalibrated baseline, as intended,
but in this run the model's stubbornness was so complete that the
corrected scores lost their ability to discriminate correct from
incorrect predictions (PRR\,$= 0$).

\section*{Discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The central finding of this experiment is that the test model
(\texttt{qwen2.5:3b}) exhibited maximal parametric stubbornness
($\Psi = 1.0$): it produced identical confidence scores of $0.9000$
regardless of whether it was conditioned on a current or an outdated
context. This caused the TCC correction to collapse to a fixed output
($\hat{p}_{\mathrm{TCC}} = 0.0000$), eliminating its discriminative
power and yielding AUROC\,$= 0.4895$ and PRR\,$= 0$. The statistical
tests confirm this is not noise: TCC-UQ scores are systematically and
significantly different from the baseline ($D = 0.713$,
$p \approx 4.90\times10^{-37}$; $t = -64.48$,
$p \approx 9.66\times10^{-111}$), but the direction of the shift
(uniformly downward) does not correlate with correctness. These results highlight an important boundary condition of the TCC
framework: it relies on the target model being \emph{sensitive} to
context switching. When a model is fully stubborn, the causal contrast
in~(\ref{eq:stubbornness}) carries no information, and no post-hoc
correction can recover it. Future work should investigate (i) context
sensitivity probing before applying TCC, (ii) alternative causal
contrast functions that do not degenerate when $\Psi = 1$, and (iii)
evaluation on models that demonstrate measurable context sensitivity.

Recent work has highlighted that standard uncertainty estimation
methods often fall short in RAG settings because they struggle to handle
conflicts between a model's internal parametric knowledge and the 
non-parametric retrieved context~\cite{soudani2025uncertainty}. We formalize this conflict 
through our introduced concept of parametric stubbornness. The stubbornness score $\Psi$ defined in~(\ref{eq:stubbornness}) is
useful on its own, because it separates two different problems:
noise coming from the retrieved documents (retrieval-induced
uncertainty), and the model's reluctance to update its beliefs
(epistemic stubbornness). This distinction aligns with recent calls to
reexamine the aleatoric--epistemic dichotomy in the context of
retrieval-augmented systems~\cite{kirchhof2025reexamining}.
Treating these separately opens the door to more targeted fixes for
each one, complementing approaches such as Textual Bayes~\cite{ross2025textual}
and feature-gap analysis for epistemic uncertainty quantification~\cite{bakman2025uncertainty}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section*{Conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We introduced TCC-UQ, a framework that (i) measures how stubbornly a
model ignores new evidence using causal tools, (ii) evaluates
calibration through ECE and reliability diagrams, and (iii) uses
non-parametric statistical tests to confirm that the resulting
confidence distribution is statistically distinct from an uncalibrated
baseline. In this experimental run on \texttt{qwen2.5:3b}, TCC-UQ
achieved an AUROC of $0.4895$, an ECE of $0.1337$, and a PRR of $0$,
with a measured stubbornness of $\Psi = 1.0$ indicating that the target
model was entirely insensitive to temporal context changes. The
statistical tests ($D = 0.713$, $p \approx 4.90\times10^{-37}$;
$t = -64.48$, $p \approx 9.66\times10^{-111}$) confirm that the TCC
correction produces a significantly different score distribution, though
discriminative power was lost due to maximal stubbornness. Future work
will focus on model selection criteria and robustified causal contrast
functions to extend TCC-UQ to fully stubborn models, while retaining its
low memory footprint ($2.1$\,MB) and transparent causal interpretability.
