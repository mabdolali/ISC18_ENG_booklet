\section{Method for Estimating Genotype Stability, Adaptability, and
Environmental Discrimination in Plant Breeding
Programs}

\textbf{Naser Sabaghnia}\footnote{\begin{quote}
  \textbf{Corresponding Author:} sabaghnia@maragheh.ac.ir
  \end{quote}}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{University of Maragheh, Iran}
\end{quote}

\textbf{Abstract:}

Breeding programs aim not only to improve crop performance but also to
ensure stability and adaptability across diverse environments.
Evaluating genotype resilience requires understanding both general
adaptability ability (GAA), the mean trait performance across multiple
environments, as well as specific adaptability ability (SAA), the
deviation of a genotype in a particular environment. A comprehensive
approach for assessing GAA, SAA, and genotype stability, is presented
for integrating both linear and nonlinear responses to environmental
variation. The method relies on multi-environment trials, partitioning
phenotypic variance into genotype, environment, and genotype ×
environment interaction components using ANOVA models, and calculating
stability indices such as the relative stability and stability value of
genotypes (SVG). This framework allows breeders to select genotypes with
optimal performance while maintaining stability, balancing trade-offs
between high mean trait values and environmental sensitivity.
Furthermore, it provides insights into environmental differentiation and
the compensatory or destabilizing effects of genotype × environment
interactions. By combining theoretical and practical aspects of adaptive
breeding, this approach offers a robust tool for guiding selection
strategies that enhance both productivity and resilience in crop
populations. To compare populations and select appropriate breeding
methods across different environments, a technique is introduced to
partition the phenotypic variance of a population into GAA and SAA
variances.

\textbf{Keywords:} Adaptability ability, Stability value, Genotype ×
environment interaction

\section{Introduction}

Plant breeders are challenged not only with the task of increasing plant
performance (productivity), but also with combining performance with
resistance to biotic and abiotic environmental conditions. Accordingly,
researchers have focused on studying and developing methods to evaluate
resilience (stability, general and specific adaptability of genotypes),
as well as breeding approaches aimed at optimizing these important plant
traits. Various approaches exist for estimating genotype stability. The
terms general and specific adaptability (Annicchiarico, 2002), which
reflect the overall response of a genotype across all environments and
its specific response in a particular environment, have been reported in
the literature. Studying genotypes across different environments can
also provide information about the environments themselves as a context
for breeding. Kilchevskii \& Khotilova, (1985), proposed a genetic
analysis method based on testing genotypes in multiple environments that
allows researchers to determine the general and specific adaptability
and stability of genotypes, and to compare environments in terms of
their ability to differentiate genotypes. In this approach, the breeding
value of a genotype can be estimated depending on the breeding
objectives, and the breeding process can be guided according to
adaptability levels. This method also enables the partitioning of a
population\textquotesingle s phenotypic variance into general and
specific adaptability variances, allowing comparisons among populations
and informed selection of breeding methods.

\section{General and specific adaptability
ability}

Adaptability is defined as the ability of a genotype to maintain its
specific phenotypic trait under a particular environmental condition.
General adaptability ability (GAA) of a genotype represents the mean
trait value across different environmental conditions, while specific
adaptability ability (SAA) represents the deviation from GAA in a
specific environment. The method for evaluating GAA and SAA is based on
testing a population of n genotypes across m environments. The number of
replications is k. Accordingly; \emph{x\textsubscript{ikr}} is the
phenotypic value of genotype i in replication r in environment k;
\emph{u} is the overall mean of all phenotypic values;
\emph{v\textsubscript{i}} is the effect of genotype i;
\emph{d\textsubscript{k}} is the effect of environment k;
\emph{(vd)\textsubscript{ik}} is the interaction effect of genotype i
with environment k; \emph{e\textsubscript{ikr}} is the random error
associated with phenotype ikr.

To determine the significance of genotype, environment, and genotype ×
environment interaction effects on the phenotypic variation of the
population, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is used.
Interpretation of the ANOVA results depends on the underlying
assumptions of the study. If genotypes and environments are non-random
samples, their effects are considered fixed, and information can be
obtained about specific genotypes and environments. In this case, Model
1 is used, in which all effect values, except for the independent
variable \emph{e\textsubscript{ikr}} are considered fixed. The variable
\emph{e\textsubscript{ikr}} is assumed to be normally distributed with
mean zero and variance σ\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{e}. The F-test
is used to determine the significance of genotype, environment, and
interaction effects. In this test, the corresponding mean squares are
compared to the mean square of the random deviations. If genotypes are a
random sample from the population and environments are a random sample
from all possible environments, then Model 2 is used. In this case, it
is possible to obtain information about the population parameters of
genotypes for a specific set of environments. In Model 2, only the
overall mean u is considered fixed, while the other effects are
independently and normally distributed with mean zero and variance
σ\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{θ}. In this case, to test hypotheses
regarding genotypes and environments, the mean square of genotype ×
environment interaction is used, and to estimate the genotype ×
environment interaction effect, the mean square of random deviations is
applied.

\section{Description of method}

Regarding accept the assumptions of Model 1, the effect of the general
adaptability ability (GAA) of genotype i is equal to u+v\emph{i}. The
deviation from the sum u+v\emph{i} represents the specific adaptability
ability effect (SAA\emph{\textsubscript{ik}}) of genotype i in
environment k. This effect consists of a linear component (the effect of
environment dk) and a nonlinear component (the interaction effect
(vd)\textsubscript{ik}). To determine the effects of
GAA\textsubscript{i} and SAA\textsubscript{ik}, the mean phenotypic
values across replications are calculated. In this way, the results of
testing n genotypes across m environments can be arranged in an n×m
table, where: x\textsubscript{i} is the sum of all phenotypic values for
genotype i, x\textsubscript{k} is the total phenotypic sum in
environment k, X.. is the grand total of all phenotypic values,
x\textsubscript{ik} is the phenotypic value of genotype i in environment
k. The GAA and SAA effects are calculated using the following formulas
(Fig. 1):

\[x_{i.} = \sum_{k}^{}x_{ik} = x_{i1} + x_{i2} + \ldots + x_{im}\]

\[x_{nk} = \sum_{k}^{}x_{ik} = x_{1k} + x_{2k} + \ldots + x_{nk}\]

\[x_{ik} = u + v_{i} + d_{k} + {(vd)}_{ik}\]

\[x_{ik} = u + {GAA}_{i} + {SAA}_{ik}\]

The variance of random deviations for the mean phenotypic value in a
specific environment x\textsubscript{ik} is computed as:

\[{var}_{xik} = \frac{\sigma_{e}^{2}}{c} = \sigma^{2}\]

The variance of the difference between the means of two genotypes is
determined as:

\[{var}_{(x_{ik}/x_{nm})} = 2\sigma^{2}\]

To obtain the standard error of the differences between effects, the
square root of the corresponding variance must be taken. The least
significant difference (LSD) is then obtained by multiplying the
standard error by a constant magnitude. Comparison of genotypes in terms
of general adaptability can be performed by comparing
GAA\textsubscript{i} values. To estimate the interaction strength of
genotype i with environments, the genotype × environment interaction
variance can be used. To measure the stability of genotype i, it is
recommended to use the variance of SAA\textsubscript{i}. Therefore,
stability can be defined as the capacity of a genotype to maintain its
phenotypic expression under various environmental conditions through
organized mechanisms.

\section{Stability analysis}

Stabilizing selection provides the evolutionary basis for developing
organized mechanisms that ensure the phenotypic stability of genotypes.
In this way, stability in the expression of one trait can be combined
with flexibility in the expression of other traits. The proposed method
for estimating stability is based on combining the linear and nonlinear
components of the genotype's response to the environment. This approach
differs from other established methods like regression model of Finlay
and Wilkinson (1963), in which stability is measured using a linear
response; ecovalence in which stability is estimated via a nonlinear
response; and regression models of Eberhart \& Russell (1966), in which
parameters for both linear and nonlinear genotype responses are
determined.

The concept of relative genotype stability is defined as follows:

\[s_{gi} = \frac{\sigma_{{GAA}_{i}}}{u + {SAA}_{i}} \times 100\]

This index allows comparison of experimental results obtained across
different sets of crops, genotypes, environments, and traits under
study. Essentially, relative stability is analogous to the coefficient
of variation when studying a genotype across multiple environments. The
ratio l\textsubscript{gi} can serve as a nonlinear index of genotype i's
response to the environment:

\[l_{gi} = \frac{\sigma_{GE}^{2}}{\sigma_{SAA}^{2}}\]

If lgi→1, the genotype exhibits mostly nonlinear responses across most
environments, while if lgi→0, the genotype's response to the environment
is predominantly linear. The response of a genotype to improved
environmental conditions can be quantified using the regression
coefficient of the genotype across environments. There is considerable
disagreement among breeders regarding the interpretation of genotype
stability. Consequently, Eberhart and Russell (1966), suggested that an
ideal genotype should have: a high mean trait value, a regression
coefficient close to 1, and the smallest deviation from the regression
line. However, the work of Mulder et al. (2007), demonstrated that
through breeding, any combination of mean trait value and environmental
sensitivity can be achieved. The most acceptable perspective is that of
Finlay and Wilkinson (1963), regression model which is defined an
optimal genotype as one that possesses, as high general adaptability,
maximum performance under favorable environments, and maximum stability.
According to Martínez-Peña et al. (2023), a genotype with moderate but
stable yield has higher economic value than a genotype with high
potential but highly variable yield. Similarly, Bailey-Serres et al.
(2019) reported that in crop production, only 30-50\% of the potential
productivity of dense genotypes is realized, primarily due to
insufficient environmental stability.

The final evaluation of a breeding genotype depends on the objectives of
adaptive breeding (Fig. 2). The main directions for adaptive breeding
are; breeding genotypes for specific adaptability (SAA) in a particular
environment. Such selection is only desirable under controlled and
managed environmental conditions (e.g., greenhouse, irrigated field). At
the same time, there is a risk of erosion of the genetic basis of
general viability and reduced genotype stability. Breeding genotypes for
general adaptability (GAA) across multiple environments, selection is
based on the mean phenotypic value across all environments. According to
Omel'yanyuk (2013), this approach achieves maximum trait enhancement
compared to selection in favorable or unfavorable environments, while
providing moderate environmental sensitivity. The latter is not
controlled during the breeding process.

\section{Breeding for GAA while considering
stability}

The theoretical basis of this adaptive breeding approach is concerning
the inseparable forms of natural selection: directional and stabilizing.
The first form alters the structure and performance of organisms, while
the second stabilizes morphogenesis, leading to automated ontogeny and
the development of regulatory mechanisms. The effectiveness of
stabilizing selection in plants has been demonstrated in several studies
(Cresswell, 1998; Rolhauser \& Pucheta, 2017). However, stabilizing
selection alone cannot create new traits; it is only important for
preserving valuable population characteristics and increasing resistance
to environmental fluctuations. Breeding for GAA with consideration of
stability requires a specific selection criterion, which allows the
combination of performance and stability in the genotype.

Kilchevskii \& Khotilova, (1985), proposed the stability value genotype
(SVG) as:

\[{SVG}_{gi} = \frac{\sqrt{\left( \sum_{}^{}{({SAA}_{gi})}^{2} \right) - (\frac{MSE}{df})}}{u + {SAA}_{i}} \times 100\]

In practice, applying the usual selection criterion p=2 is unlikely,
because for large values of σ\textsubscript{SAAi}, negative SVG values
may occur, and some fertility genes could be lost. As a first step
toward achieving an optimal balance between performance and stability in
breeding, we suggest using the population mean SVG equal to 0.5 grand
mean. This facilitates establishing the relationship between the average
relative stability of genotypes and the value of p. In this approach, at
the lowest value, selection prioritizes stability, while at the highest
value, selection favors performance more intensely. Environmental
evaluation is also crucial in breeding. Environments can be classified
into three types; stable, in which population polymorphism does not
appear; analytical, which helps identify different biotypes; harmonized,
where the viability of different biotypes is constrained and differences
among them are equalized. The above analysis method allows not only the
evaluation of genotype adaptability parameters, but also defining the
environment as a breeding context.

When interpreting SAA and GAA variances, a question arises: how
informative are they, and can they replace genotype × environment
interaction variance? Some researchers believe that genotype ×
environment interaction variance can serve as a measure of genotype
stability. However, statistical evaluation does not reflect all
biological realities of genotype × environment interaction, including
increase or decrease in genotype (or environment) effects. If the signs
of genotype (environment) effects and the interaction do not match, the
variance of environmental differentiation or genotype stability
(compensation effect) decreases. If the signs are aligned, the
corresponding variance increases (destabilizing effect). Thus, even if
genotype × environment interaction variance is identical, the variance
of genotype stability and the variance of environmental differentiation
may differ. The practical importance of the compensation/destabilization
effect lies in the unpredictability of genotype stability or
environmental differentiation when using
σ\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{GE}, as a criterion for selecting
genotypes or environments. Therefore, SAA and GAA variances provide more
objective information on genotype stability and environmental
differentiation compared to genotype × environment interaction variance.

\section{Conclusion}

This study introduces an integrated framework for evaluating genotype
adaptability and stability based on general adaptability ability (GAA)
and specific adaptability ability (SAA). By partitioning phenotypic
variation into general and environment-specific components, the approach
provides a more informative assessment of genotype performance across
environments than conventional GE interaction analysis alone. The use of
GAA, SAA, and stability indices enables simultaneous selection for high
yield and stability, supporting more effective breeding decisions.
